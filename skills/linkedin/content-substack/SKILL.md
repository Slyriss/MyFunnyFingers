# Substack Content Skill

## Step 0: Always Load Voice First

Before doing anything else, load `voice-profile/SKILL.md`. All voice rules apply here — no em dashes, no buzzwords, specific detail over adjectives, conviction over hedging. The rules do not relax because the format is longer.

## Reference: The Top 5 Substacks and What They Teach

These are the five largest or most structurally instructive Substacks by subscriber count. Each one teaches a specific technique. Apply the relevant ones when writing Sean's essays.

### 1. Letters from an American — Heather Cox Richardson (2.9M+ subscribers)
**What she does:** Opens with today's specific event. Widens immediately into historical frame. Shows the current moment as part of a longer story the reader didn't know they were in.
**The technique:** *Reframe through wider lens.* The personal or immediate becomes meaningful when placed inside a larger arc — historical, cultural, or structural. The insight is not "here is what happened." It's "here is what this is an instance of."
**Apply when:** Sean has an experience in Shenzhen, or at an SME, or in a boardroom, that reflects a bigger shift. Start with the specific. Pull back to reveal what it belongs to.

### 2. The Pragmatic Engineer — Gergely Orosz (top paid tech newsletter)
**What he does:** Writes as an insider for insiders. Long-form deep dives from someone who has worked inside the rooms he writes about. No fluff. Every sentence carries weight. Respects reader's time by never hedging or padding.
**The technique:** *Insider-to-peer voice.* Not teacher to student. Not observer to audience. Peer to peer — here is what I saw when I was in the room. The authority comes from presence, not credentials.
**Apply when:** Sean is writing about tech, building, AI, enterprise vs startup, or anything where he has direct access others don't. Write as someone who was there, not someone who read about it.

### 3. Lenny's Newsletter — Lenny Rachitsky (500K+ subscribers, $1M+/year)
**What he does:** Hook → Context → Tactical breakdown. 1:1 writing, casual and fluff-free. Spends 10–100 hours per piece. High signal-to-noise. Goes through 5–6 drafts. Every sentence earns its place.
**The technique:** *Earn the payoff.* The hook promises something. The context earns the reader's trust. The breakdown delivers. Do not skip steps. A good takeaway that arrives too early is wasted.
**Apply when:** Sean is writing about process, decisions, or lessons from work. Lay the ground. Build the tension. Then land.

### 4. The Free Press — Bari Weiss (170K+ paid subscribers, $10M+/year)
**What she does:** States the thesis in sentence one. No hedging. Opinion declared plainly, then backed with reporting, interviews, and evidence. "Honest. Independent. Fearless."
**The technique:** *Conviction in line one.* The reader should know exactly what the essay argues before the third sentence. Uncertainty, ambiguity, and "on the other hand" belong inside the argument — not in the opening.
**Apply when:** Sean has a real opinion. Not a take for its own sake, but something he actually believes that most people in his world don't say. Open with the claim. Spend the essay earning it.

### 5. The Profile — Polina Pompliano (six figures annually)
**What she does:** Profiles extraordinary people through one defining moment or decision. Draws on Gay Talese's technique — vivid, specific, scene-based. Finds the detail everyone else missed and builds the piece around it.
**The technique:** *One detail does more than three paragraphs.* Do not describe someone as driven or brilliant. Find the scene — the $1,000/month cockroach exterminator who became a Navy SEAL — and let it do the work. The specific is always more powerful than the summarized.
**Apply when:** Sean is writing a portrait post (someone else as subject). Build the piece around one moment or decision. Everything else is context for that moment.

## Step 1: Intake — Before Writing Anything

When Sean gives a raw input, do not write the essay immediately.

First, identify what type of essay the input is closest to (one sentence, why). Then ask the following targeted questions. The goal is to arrive at the central argument and the turning point — without these, the essay has no spine.

**Questions to always ask:**

1. **What is the central argument in one sentence?** Not what happened — what you think it means. If Sean can't answer in one sentence, the essay isn't ready yet.

2. **What changed your thinking?** Was there a specific moment, conversation, scene, or piece of information that shifted something? Where were you? What exactly did you see or hear?

3. **What would a smart peer in your world probably believe about this topic before reading your essay?** What are you disagreeing with, complicating, or replacing?

**Ask additional questions by essay type:**

**Field Report** — What is the specific scene that opens the piece? What time of day, what were you doing? What did you notice that others might have missed?

**Argument** — What's the strongest counterargument? Has Sean tried to steel-man it? What evidence from his own experience makes him confident anyway?

**Portrait** — What is the one decision or moment that defines this person? What does Sean know about them that isn't in their official bio or public narrative?

**Essay (reflective)** — What did Sean believe before the experience that turned out to be wrong? What is the gap between the expectation and the reality?

Do not ask more than 4 questions total. Ask only what is genuinely missing. If Sean's input is already specific, skip the relevant question.

## Sub-Skill: Research

### When to trigger

Run this sub-skill automatically before writing. Do not ask Sean whether to research — assess the essay type and topic, then decide.

**Always research:**
- Argument essays — the claim needs grounding beyond personal experience
- Field Reports where the personal experience reflects a documented trend (e.g., Shenzhen tech growth, SEA startup funding, Singapore SME IT adoption)

**Research if useful:**
- Reflective essays where one grounding fact would deepen the insight without making it clinical (e.g., the chicken rice essay could carry a single line about Singapore's hawker culture receiving UNESCO recognition — it deepens the point about invisible standards without becoming a food industry report)
- Portrait essays where the subject's context involves a verifiable claim

**Do not research:**
- Pure personal narrative where the evidence is lived experience and data would feel imported
- When Sean's presence in the room is the authority — insider essays do not need external validation
- When adding a stat would interrupt the rhythm of a paragraph that is already working

---

### What to look up by topic area

**Singapore / culture:**
- Hawker culture: UNESCO intangible cultural heritage (designated 2023), number of licensed hawker stalls, average price stability over decades
- Economy: GDP per capita, SME share of GDP, ranking in global competitiveness indices
- Education / talent pipeline: NUS rankings, NOC program scale and outcomes

**China / Shenzhen:**
- Shenzhen GDP growth rate, patent filings per capita, manufacturing-to-tech transition timeline
- Pearl River Delta tech cluster scale vs Silicon Valley comparisons
- Speed of infrastructure build (e.g., how fast Shenzhen went from fishing village to tech hub)

**SEA tech ecosystem:**
- Startup funding by year (Indonesia, Singapore, Vietnam dominant), unicorn count
- Digital economy size (Google/Temasek/Bain e-Conomy SEA report is the canonical source)
- SME digitization rates across SEA

**Enterprise / AI:**
- AI adoption rates in SMEs vs large enterprise
- GenAI enterprise deployment figures (Gartner, McKinsey Global Institute)
- SAP market share in ERP, customer count

**Physical performance (if relevant):**
- Hyrox participation growth, race counts, category standards
- Sub-25 min 5K context (top 10-15% of recreational runners globally)

---

### How to use data inside the essay

**One stat, one sentence, woven in.** Never announce a stat. Never write "According to X" unless the source itself is the point.

Do this:
> Singapore has over 6,000 licensed hawker stalls. Most of them are run by people who spent years learning one dish.

Not this:
> According to the Singapore Food Agency, there are approximately 6,000+ hawker stalls across Singapore's 119 hawker centres and food courts.

**The stat should surprise or reframe — not just decorate.** If removing the stat doesn't weaken the sentence, cut it.

**Two stats per essay is the limit.** Three is almost always one too many. If you have three candidates, keep the two most surprising.

**Round numbers are fine.** "More than 6,000" is better than "6,247." Precision signals a report. Rounding signals a writer who has done their homework and trusts the reader.

**Never fabricate.** If the exact figure is uncertain, write around it: "tens of thousands" or "the numbers have barely moved in thirty years." A hedged true statement is better than a confident false one.

---

### Research → intake integration

If research surfaces a figure that would sharpen one of the intake questions, flag it before asking. Example:

> Before I ask about your central argument — Singapore's hawker culture received UNESCO intangible cultural heritage status in 2023. That might be worth weaving in if the essay goes toward the standards angle. Keep that in mind when you answer.

Do not pad the intake with research findings. Surface one fact if it's genuinely useful. Otherwise, research quietly and use it in the draft.

## Essay Types

### Field Report
Writing from inside an ongoing or recent experience — Shenzhen, an SME engagement, a competition, a conversation with a founder. Opens with a scene. Builds to a reframe. The experience is the evidence. The conclusion changes how the reader sees something they thought they already understood.
**Length:** 700–1,100 words
**Structure:** Scene → tension or question → evidence from inside → reframe → clean close

### Argument
A real opinion, stated in sentence one. No wind-up. The body earns the claim through evidence — specific, lived, not theoretical. Acknowledges the strongest counterargument, then explains why Sean disagrees anyway. Closes on conviction, not a question.
**Length:** 600–900 words
**Structure:** Claim → evidence → counterargument addressed → conviction close

### Portrait
One person as subject, told through one defining decision or moment. Not a biography. Not a listicle of their accomplishments. One moment that reveals something true about who they are and why it matters.
**Length:** 800–1,200 words
**Structure:** Scene (the defining moment) → who they are before it → why the decision was hard → what it reveals → what it means beyond them

### Essay (reflective)
Slower burn. Explores a tension or gap between expectation and reality. Builds through multiple scenes or beats. The conclusion is hard-won — the reader should feel the essay earned it, not declared it. Closest to Richardson's historical reframe technique.
**Length:** 900–1,500 words
**Structure:** Opening image or scene → tension introduced → scenes that complicate → turning point → hard-won conclusion

## Format Rules

- **No headers.** No bullets. No bold text inside the essay body.
- **Section breaks** (three dashes or a single blank line between sections) are allowed for Field Reports and Essays. Use sparingly — no more than 3 breaks per essay.
- **Paragraph length:** Mix short (1–2 sentence) and medium (3–4 sentence) paragraphs. A single sentence on its own line is a valid rhetorical move — use it once per essay, at the most important line.
- **No em dashes.** Same rule as LinkedIn. Replace with a period or a new sentence.
- **No hashtags.** Substack is not a discovery platform.
- **No buzzwords.** Same banned list as voice profile.
- **No closing question.** End on a statement. The reader should leave with something, not a prompt.
- **No "I learned that" or "The lesson is."** The essay should demonstrate the insight, not announce it.

## Writing the Essay

After intake questions are answered:

1. Run the Research sub-skill. Identify 0–2 facts that would sharpen the essay. Hold them until the right moment in the draft.
2. Write the essay in full. Do not stop to ask for approval mid-draft.
3. Apply the relevant technique from the top 5 Substacks based on the essay type.
4. Open with a specific scene or a plain claim — never with context-setting or background.
5. The payload lands in the final third of the essay. Build toward it.
6. Weave any research findings in as single sentences inside existing paragraphs. Never drop them as standalone lines.
7. Close on a single strong statement. No questions. No summary. No "and that's why."

## Quality Check Before Delivering

Before outputting the draft, verify:

- [ ] Voice profile rules applied (no em dashes, no buzzwords, specific detail over adjectives)
- [ ] One central argument — not two
- [ ] Opens with scene or claim, not background
- [ ] No line that could have been written by someone who wasn't there
- [ ] Closes on a statement, not a question
- [ ] Format rules followed (no headers, no bullets, no bold)
- [ ] Any stats used are verified, woven in as prose, and limited to two maximum
- [ ] No stat announced with "According to" or floated as a standalone line

If any item fails, fix it before delivering.
