# Viral LinkedIn Carousel Patterns — Research Notes

Compiled June 2026 from Sarah Hart's analysis of 11 viral carousels (each 800+ likes within 90 days, 7.5 hours of study) — including direct study of her annotated slide breakdowns — plus current LinkedIn format guidance.

## The numbers (Sarah Hart study)

| Metric | Finding |
|---|---|
| Most common slide count | **11** |
| Words per slide | **20-30** (the mode — see range below) |
| Words per line | **5-7** |

Industry-wide engagement data agrees: 8-12 slides is the sweet spot for dwell time, saves, and shares. Drop-off accelerates after slide 10; a brilliant 4-slide carousel beats a mediocre 15-slide one.

## Per-creator data (from Hart's annotated breakdowns)

| Creator | Pages | Words/page | Reactions | Comments | Standout patterns |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sahil Bloom | 11 | 87 | 9,141 | 694 | Brutalist all-type slides, wild size mixing, circled/struck-through words, infographic style, CTA = newsletter + screenshot of it + background color change |
| Colby Kultgen | 11 | 34 | 2,641 | 333 | First page rehooks reader, numbered headings (#1, #2…), every page has an illustration, yellow-highlighted key lines, CTA = opt-in in bio + screenshot |
| Luke Matthews | 13 | 18.6 | 1,079 | 936 | "STEP ONE/TWO…" headers, every page has a real screenshot example with arrows, recap slide before CTA, CTA hints at what's coming next |
| Nick Broekma | 15 | 30 | 1,922 | 424 | First 3 pages rehook reader, unique hand-drawn arrows and callouts, real copy examples, CTA = follow + book a call + profile pic |
| Chris Do | 10 | 27.5 | 3,477 | 216 | Editorial poster design, typographic-art slide (LOVE/HATE), yellow highlights, full-bleed photo slide, CTA = a question + graphic |

Takeaway from the spread: words-per-page ranged **18.6 to 87**. Density is fine *if* the typography is doing the design work (Sahil Bloom's 87-wpp deck was the biggest hit of the set). Low density needs an image or example on every page to carry it. What never varies: one dominant idea per slide, heavy size contrast, branding on every page, explicit CTA.

## Format specs

- **1080 x 1350 px portrait (4:5)** — portrait takes more feed real estate than square
- Uploaded as a **PDF document post** (not images), keep under 10 MB
- Text must be legible at phone size: minimum ~28px body, headlines 90px+
- Each slide consumed in 2-3 seconds of swiping

## Cover slides (hooks) that worked

Real examples from the study's viral set:
- "6 Copywriting Lessons That Made Me 6 Figures" (Lara Acosta) — number + personal proof
- "Company pages are dead" (John Harrison) — contrarian declaration; noted for brilliant **size contrast** in design
- "My anti-to-do list" (Sahil Bloom) — pattern inversion / curiosity

Cover slide anatomy (all 11 viral carousels used a two-tier system):
- **Hook + sub-hook**: a primary hook placed prominently (top/center), then a secondary line adding context or intrigue
- **Personal branding on the cover**: creator name at top, or photo mid-slide (Sahil Bloom, John Harrison) — faces and names build trust at the scroll moment; logos, if used at all, go small at bottom-middle
- Bold specific claim, stat, or question — never a logo-only or generic title slide
- Value obvious in a single glance; swipe indicator (arrow, "swipe →") signals more content

## Inside pages

- **Dive right in**: slide 2 goes straight into the content. No agenda, no "introduction", no throat-clearing — the cover already made the promise, the next swipe must start paying it off
- **…but the best decks "rehook" while diving in**: Colby Kultgen's slide 2 and Nick Broekma's slides 2-4 deepen the curiosity (raise the stakes, sharpen the promise) *while* delivering the first real content. Rehooking and diving in are the same move done well, not alternatives
- One idea per slide; the slide headline IS the idea
- **Numbered structure**: "#1 / #2…" or "STEP ONE / STEP TWO…" headings appeared across the winners — they make progress legible and the carousel feel completable
- **Unique text styles carry the design**: mixed font pairings, varied weights and sizes *within* a headline, yellow/accent highlighter on key lines (Kultgen, Chris Do), circled or struck-through words (Bloom), even typographic art slides (Chris Do's LOVE/HATE wall). Style individual words, not whole slides
- **Two design schools, both viral** — pick one per deck and commit:
  1. *Typographic*: all-type slides, brutalist size contrast, density OK (Bloom at 87 wpp, Chris Do)
  2. *Illustrated*: low word count with an image, illustration, or real screenshot example on every single page (Kultgen at 34 wpp, Matthews at 18.6 wpp)
- **Evidence elements**: real screenshots with annotation arrows substantiate every claim (Matthews put one on every page); statistics often sit at the bottom of a slide to reinforce the point with authority
- **Hand-drawn arrows and callouts** (Broekma) point at the part of an example that matters — they read as human, not template
- **Branding on every page**: handle or name in a consistent corner of all slides, not just cover and CTA
- **Padding is critical** (Hart's own tip): keep text away from edges, and especially away from the bottom corners and sides — LinkedIn overlays its navigation arrows there and will cover your text
- Arrows pointing toward the next slide (Matt Barker's pattern) guide progression; progress markers ("3/11") keep swipers oriented
- A full-bleed photo slide (Chris Do's Mike Tyson portrait) makes a powerful pattern-break mid-deck
- Dense low-contrast slides were called "headache" inducing by readers
- Open loops at the bottom of slides ("but there's a catch →") earn the next swipe

## Inside pages

- One idea per slide; the slide headline IS the idea
- Size contrast is the dominant design move in winners — huge headline, tiny support text
- Low contrast / dense slides were explicitly called out as "headache" inducing by readers
- Progress markers ("3/11") keep swipers oriented and create completion pull
- Open loops at the bottom of slides ("but there's a catch →") earn the next swipe

## CTA patterns

- Final slide always has an explicit CTA: follow, comment, share, save
- **Comment-gated lead magnets** are the highest-engagement pattern observed: "Comment 'template' and I'll DM you the checklist" — comments are the heaviest-weighted algorithm signal (Luke Matthews' deck had fewer reactions than others but 936 comments)
- A **recap slide** before the CTA (Matthews) summarizes the steps and boosts saves
- CTA variants seen in the viral set:
  - Newsletter plug *with a screenshot of the newsletter itself* as proof (Bloom)
  - Opt-in link in bio + screenshot of the lead magnet (Kultgen)
  - Follow + book-a-call + the creator's profile picture for a human close (Broekma)
  - A reflective question + bold graphic (Chris Do) — questions farm comments
  - A teaser for what's coming next ("launching something Friday…") (Matthews)
- The CTA slide breaks the visual pattern — background color change or inverted theme signals the end (Bloom)
- Carousels ending abruptly without a CTA waste earned attention

## Caption (post text) patterns

- First line is a second hook, worded differently from slide 1, under ~140 chars (truncation point)
- Short lines, generous line breaks — wall-of-text captions suppress swiping
- The caption CTA mirrors the final-slide CTA
- 3-5 specific hashtags; hashtag walls look spammy and don't help reach
